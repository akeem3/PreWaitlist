# Pre-Launch Waitlist Tool — Feedback & Customer Discovery System

> **Purpose:** The operating system for learning from users after acquisition, separating signal from noise, and converting feedback into product decisions and evidence.
>
> **Product:** Pre-Launch Waitlist Tool  
> **Primary market:** US founders / indie hackers  
> **Stage:** MVP → first users → product-market learning

---

# 1. Feedback Is Not a Feature Request Inbox

The biggest mistake is treating feedback as:

```text
User asks for feature
↓
Add feature
```

Instead:

```text
User behavior
+
User words
+
Observed friction
+
Product analytics
+
Customer interviews
↓
Underlying opportunity/problem
↓
Hypothesis
↓
Experiment
↓
Evidence
↓
Product decision
```

Modern continuous-discovery practice emphasizes ongoing customer contact, synthesizing what is learned, connecting feedback to opportunities/outcomes, and testing assumptions rather than simply collecting requests. citeturn1search14turn1search16

---

# 2. The Core Feedback Question

Never ask only:

> “What feature do you want?”

Ask:

> **“What were you trying to accomplish, what happened, and what did you do next?”**

This produces behavioral evidence rather than wish lists.

Teresa Torres' continuous-discovery framework emphasizes interviewing to uncover unmet needs, pain points and desires, then testing solutions against those opportunities. citeturn1search11turn1search17

---

# 3. Separate the Feedback Layer From Marketing

## Marketing layer

Answers:

> **How do we get the right founders to try the product?**

Inputs:

- Reddit
- X
- LinkedIn
- direct outreach
- Product Hunt
- HN
- content

Outputs:

- visitors
- signups
- activated founders

---

## Feedback layer

Answers:

> **What happens after they arrive, and why?**

Inputs:

- product behavior
- interviews
- feedback button
- support messages
- complaints
- analytics
- churn
- upgrade behavior

Outputs:

- product improvements
- better onboarding
- better positioning
- better messaging
- evidence
- case studies

# 4. The Feedback Loop

Use:

```text
ACQUIRE
   ↓
ACTIVATE
   ↓
OBSERVE
   ↓
INTERVIEW
   ↓
SYNTHESIZE
   ↓
FORM HYPOTHESIS
   ↓
RUN EXPERIMENT
   ↓
MEASURE
   ↓
DECIDE
   ↓
SHIP / DON'T SHIP
   ↓
OBSERVE AGAIN
```

This should be continuous, not a one-time post-launch survey.

Product Talk recommends ongoing customer touchpoints and revisiting the opportunity space as interviews accumulate. citeturn1search9turn1search14

---

# 5. First 20–50 Founders

The first users deserve disproportionate attention.

Do not simply watch analytics.

Talk to them.

### Target

First:

**20–50 founders**

### Objective

Understand:

1. Why did they sign up?
2. What were they trying to launch?
3. What did they expect?
4. What confused them?
5. What did they do first?
6. Where did they hesitate?
7. What did they ignore?
8. What feature produced the strongest reaction?
9. What made them return?
10. What would make them pay?

---

# 6. When To Ask For Feedback

Do not ask everyone the same question at the same time.

Trigger feedback around behavior.

## After signup

> “What are you hoping to accomplish with this waitlist?”

## After publishing

> “What were you expecting to happen after publishing?”

## After first subscriber

> “Did seeing the first signup give you the signal you expected?”

## After several subscribers

> “What are you checking most often?”

## After using qualification

> “Did the answers tell you anything you didn't already know?”

## After using warmth

> “Does the Hot/Warm/Cold view change what you would do next?”

## Before cancellation

> “What made you decide this wasn't worth continuing?”

Behavior-linked questions are more useful than generic satisfaction surveys.

---

# 7. In-App Feedback System

Keep the mechanism extremely simple.

### Feedback button

Possible categories:

```text
🐛 Bug
😕 Confusing
💡 Idea
❤️ Love this
❌ Something is missing
💬 Other
```

Then:

> “Tell me what happened.”

Optional:

> “Can I follow up with you?”

The purpose is not to build a giant feedback database.

It is to make it **cheap for users to tell you when reality differs from your assumptions.**

---

# 8. “Talk to the Founder”

For an early-stage product, make direct founder access easy.

Possible:

> **Talk to the founder**

or:

> **Something feels wrong? Tell me.**

This creates a different channel from formal support.

Early users often provide much more useful information when they know a real builder will read it.

---

# 9. Let Users Complain

Do not design feedback only around praise.

You specifically want:

- confusion;
- frustration;
- failed expectations;
- missing functionality;
- reasons for abandonment;
- reasons for cancellation.

A complaint can reveal a broken assumption.

---

# 10. Feedback Classification

Every piece of feedback should be classified.

### Type A — Bug

The product does not behave as intended.

### Type B — UX problem

The product works but the user struggles to use it.

### Type C — Confusion

The user does not understand what something means.

### Type D — Missing capability

The product does not currently support a required job.

### Type E — Feature request

The user proposes a specific solution.

### Type F — Pricing objection

The value may not justify the price.

### Type G — Positioning problem

The user does not understand why the product is different.

### Type H — Acquisition mismatch

The person arrived but is not actually the target customer.

### Type I — Education problem

The capability exists but the user does not know how to use it.

This classification prevents:

> “User asked for X”

from automatically becoming:

> “Build X.”

---

# 11. Separate Symptoms From Opportunities

Example:

### User says:

> “Can you add a Slack integration?”

Do not immediately create:

> Slack integration

Instead ask:

> “What are you trying to accomplish with Slack?”

Maybe the real need is:

> “I need to know when an important signup happens.”

Possible solutions then include:

- email notification;
- dashboard alert;
- webhook;
- Slack integration.

The requested feature is a **solution**.

The underlying need is the **opportunity**.

Continuous discovery explicitly encourages this distinction between opportunities and solutions. citeturn1search16turn1search9

---

# 12. Interview Structure

Keep founder interviews short.

### 15–30 minutes

## Part 1 — Context

> “Tell me what you're building.”

> “When are you planning to launch?”

## Part 2 — Current behavior

> “How were you planning to collect early interest before using this?”

> “What were you using before?”

## Part 3 — Experience

> “Walk me through what you did after signing up.”

> “Where did you hesitate?”

> “What did you expect here?”

## Part 4 — Value

> “What part was most useful?”

> “What did you learn from your subscribers?”

## Part 5 — Missing value

> “What were you hoping it would do that it didn't?”

## Part 6 — Payment

> “At what point would this become something you'd actually pay for?”

Avoid leading the witness.

---

# 13. Don't Ask Hypothetical Questions Too Early

Weak:

> “Would you use feature X?”

Better:

> “Tell me about the last time you had this problem.”

Weak:

> “Would $15/month be reasonable?”

Better:

> “What are you currently paying for to solve this?”

Weak:

> “Would a Slack integration be useful?”

Better:

> “How do you currently know when something important happens in your waitlist?”

Behavior beats speculation.

# 15. Activation Definition

Do not define activation as:

> “Created an account.”

A better early activation event is closer to:

> **Founder publishes a waitlist and receives their first real subscriber.**

This demonstrates that the founder has crossed from setup into actual value.

Track the entire path:

```text
Account
→ Published
→ Shared
→ First signup
→ First insight
→ Return visit
```

Then determine empirically which event best predicts retention.

# 17. Feedback + Analytics Together

Neither is sufficient alone.

### Example

Analytics says:

> 60% of founders abandon onboarding at Step 4.

Interview says:

> “I didn't know whether I should ask qualification questions.”

Now you have:

```text
Behavioral evidence
+
Qualitative explanation
=
Strong product hypothesis
```

Potential experiment:

> Clarify the purpose of qualification before asking founders to configure it.

Then measure whether completion improves.

# 18. Build an Opportunity Map

Use a simple structure:

```text
OUTCOME
│
├── Get more qualified pre-launch demand
│
├── Understand who actually wants the product
│
├── Keep the audience engaged before launch
│
└── Turn interest into launch customers
```

Under each:

```text
Opportunity
   ↓
Observed evidence
   ↓
Possible solutions
   ↓
Assumption
   ↓
Experiment
   ↓
Result
```

This is conceptually aligned with the Opportunity Solution Tree approach: interviews uncover opportunities, while experiments evaluate possible solutions. citeturn1search9turn1search10

# 19. Evidence Strength

Not all feedback deserves equal weight.

Use an evidence hierarchy:

### Strong

- repeated behavior;
- repeated complaint from multiple target users;
- users abandoning at the same point;
- users paying for a workaround;
- users returning repeatedly;
- measurable improvement after an experiment.

### Medium

- one detailed customer interview;
- repeated qualitative comments;
- consistent support questions.

### Weak

- one feature request;
- “I'd probably use this”;
- survey preference;
- hypothetical interest;
- friend's opinion.

A loud request is not automatically strong evidence.

---

# 20. Feature Request Rule

Before building a requested feature, ask:

```text
1. Who requested it?
2. What job are they trying to accomplish?
3. How often does this problem occur?
4. How painful is it?
5. How many target users have the same problem?
6. Is there a workaround?
7. Does solving it reinforce our core positioning?
8. Can we test a smaller solution first?
```

If the answer is weak, do not build it just because someone asked.

---

# 21. Feedback Prioritization

Use a simple matrix:

| Factor        | Question                                      |
| ------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Frequency     | How often does this happen?                   |
| Severity      | How badly does it hurt?                       |
| Strategic fit | Does it reinforce the product's core promise? |
| Revenue       | Does solving it affect willingness to pay?    |
| Evidence      | How strong is the evidence?                   |
| Cost          | How expensive is the solution?                |
| Testability   | Can we test it cheaply first?                 |

This avoids roadmap-by-voting.

---

# 22. Run Small Experiments

Do not jump from:

> “Users are confused”

to:

> “Rewrite the whole onboarding.”

Test one change.

Example:

### Problem

Founders don't understand qualification questions.

### Hypothesis

A short explanation will increase configuration completion.

### Experiment

Add:

> “Ask optional questions so you can learn _who_ is joining your waitlist, not just how many.”

### Measure

```text
Step viewed
→ question configuration started
→ question configuration completed
```

If completion improves, keep it.

If not, investigate again.

# 23. Hopkins Applied to Feedback

Hopkins is useful here because he treated advertising as experimentation.

The same logic applies to product decisions:

```text
Assumption
↓
Small test
↓
Track response
↓
Compare
↓
Keep / modify / discard
```

Do not let internal opinions substitute for market evidence.

His work repeatedly emphasized test campaigns and traceable returns; the same mindset can govern product experiments. citeturn1search6turn1search15

---

# 24. Cialdini Applied to Feedback

Cialdini is primarily a persuasion framework, but some principles are useful for **how you ask for participation**.

### Reciprocity

Give users useful analysis before asking for a long interview.

### Consistency

If a founder has publicly committed to launching, ask about the specific launch journey they are already undertaking.

### Unity

Treat early founders as collaborators in building the product.

### Social proof

Share real examples of changes made because of user feedback.

Example:

> “Three founders told us X was confusing. We changed it. Here's what changed.”

Do not exaggerate the number.

# 25. Close the Feedback Loop

One of the most valuable behaviors:

```text
User reports problem
↓
You investigate
↓
You change something
↓
You tell the user
↓
You measure result
```

Example:

> “You mentioned that the qualification setup was confusing. I changed the setup flow yesterday. If you try it again, I'd love to know whether it makes more sense.”

This makes users feel heard and creates another research opportunity.

Reddit's own business guidance similarly recommends responding to feedback and keeping people updated when changes are made. citeturn0search4

# 26. Turn Feedback Into Marketing Proof

The feedback layer eventually feeds the marketing layer.

Example:

### Feedback

Founders repeatedly say:

> “I don't know which subscribers are serious.”

### Product change

Improve qualification + warmth.

### Result

Founder discovers:

> 23% of their list is highly engaged.

### Marketing asset

Case study:

> “We thought 500 signups meant 500 potential customers. After segmenting the list, we discovered 117 people were actually highly engaged.”

The cycle becomes:

```text
Marketing
↓
Users
↓
Feedback
↓
Product improvement
↓
Real result
↓
Case study
↓
Marketing
```

# 27. Weekly Feedback Operating System

Every week:

```text
MONDAY
Review analytics + support + feedback

TUESDAY
Identify strongest recurring problem

WEDNESDAY
Interview 2–5 target users

THURSDAY
Synthesize findings

FRIDAY
Choose ONE experiment

NEXT WEEK
Measure result
```

Do not try to fix everything.

One strong learning loop per week is better than a giant backlog nobody processes.

# 29. The Most Important Questions

When something isn't working:

### Acquisition

> Are we attracting the wrong people?

### Activation

> Do they understand the value?

### Usage

> Are they reaching the core experience?

### Retention

> Does the product become useful enough to return to?

### Monetization

> Is the problem painful enough to pay to solve?

### Product

> Are we solving the actual problem or merely the feature request?

---

# 30. What NOT To Do

Do not:

- build every requested feature;
- treat surveys as absolute truth;
- ask leading questions;
- interview only happy users;
- interview only churned users;
- collect feedback without synthesis;
- let the loudest customer define the roadmap;
- confuse correlation with causation;
- optimize vanity metrics;
- hide negative feedback;
- create a giant feature backlog with no evidence;
- stop talking to customers after launch.

---

# 31. The Feedback Decision Rule

Before building anything meaningful:

```text
Do we have evidence of a recurring problem?
        ↓
       NO → investigate
        ↓
       YES
        ↓
Do we understand the underlying job?
        ↓
       NO → interview
        ↓
       YES
        ↓
Can we test a smaller solution?
        ↓
       YES → experiment
        ↓
       NO → build carefully
        ↓
Measure behavior
        ↓
Keep / modify / discard
```

# 32. Final Principle

The purpose of feedback is **not to make users happy by building whatever they ask for.**

The purpose is to discover:

> **What problem is actually preventing the right customer from getting the promised outcome?**

Then:

> **Find the smallest test that can tell you what to do about it.**

Marketing tells you **who to bring in**.

Feedback tells you **what reality is saying once they arrive**.

Product development turns that evidence into changes.

And the resulting outcomes become the next marketing proof.

That is the complete loop:

```text
MARKET
  ↓
USER
  ↓
BEHAVIOR
  ↓
FEEDBACK
  ↓
INSIGHT
  ↓
EXPERIMENT
  ↓
PRODUCT
  ↓
RESULT
  ↓
PROOF
  ↓
MARKET
```
